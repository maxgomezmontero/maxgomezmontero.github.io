Measure AI against your own past decisions

A model’s accuracy score doesn’t convince a plant manager. “It agrees with what we already decided, 94% of the time, and here are the misses” does.

Most AI demos are judged on a benchmark nobody in the plant cares about. When I built tools for my own work, I wanted a number that answered the only question that matters to the people who would rely on it: would this have done what we did?

The method

  1. Collect decisions that were already made by hand. Emails that were filed, milestones that were logged, drawings that were corrected.
  2. Replay the inputs through the tool and compare its proposal with the decision on record.
  3. Sort the disagreements. Some are tool errors; some reveal that the original decision was inconsistent. Both are useful.
  4. Fix the rules, re-run, repeat. Only then let a task run in shadow mode, and only after that let it act — with a human approving writes.

For ProjectTracker that loop took email-to-project accuracy from 63% in the first version to 95%, and gave 94% agreement with my past manual decisions. I benchmarked the milestone detectors by replaying 8,771 emails — a full year of my inbox — and after go-live I audited what I had approved: 1 of 141 approved proposals had gone to the wrong project. Every ERP write still goes through an approval and a read-back.

Not all errors cost the same

The same idea runs through my wire-EDM research. Predicting surface roughness is not the goal; deciding whether to accept a part is. So we judged the network on its decisions: accepting a bad part (Type I) is far worse than flagging a good one for re-measurement (Type II). On the test set the network made zero Type I errors.

In email triage it’s the same: filing a message to the wrong project silently is worse than sending it to a review queue. Good automation is designed around that asymmetry, not around a single accuracy number.

Why this matters for anyone deploying AI

  • It uses data the organisation already has: its own history.
  • It speaks the language of the people who carry the risk.
  • It turns “do we trust the AI?” into a measured, staged rollout.
