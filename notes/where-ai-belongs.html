Where AI belongs in a plant — and where it doesn’t

A plant runs on decisions that someone must be able to trace: which job an email belongs to, whether a drawing can be released, what goes into the ERP. That shapes where I let AI in.

When I started automating my own project-engineering work, the temptation was obvious: give every email and every drawing to a language model and let it sort things out. I didn’t, and the reason is not that LLMs are bad. It’s that different parts of the job carry very different costs when they go wrong.

Three kinds of work

Rules

Decisions with a cost

Filing an email to the wrong project, releasing a drawing with the wrong hook load, writing the wrong date into the ERP. These need logic I can read, test and explain in one line.

LLM

Messy language a person will read

Summarising a long thread into one factual line, drafting a note, pulling the gist out of a vendor email. Here an LLM is excellent, and a human reads the result anyway.

Human

Writes that are hard to undo

Anything that changes a system of record. The tool proposes; I approve. Every write is re-read and verified afterwards.

Measure

Before moving the line

A task moves from “human approves” to “automatic” only after it has been measured against decisions already made by hand.

What that looks like in practice

In ProjectTracker, emails are matched to projects with 9 weighted evidence signals (a project number in the subject counts for much more than a client’s name), 13 deterministic detectors catch milestones like an accepted order or a changed ship date, and layout drawings are checked against 44 criteria. The LLM only writes a ≤160-character summary of each thread. It never sets an ID, a status or a decision. Nothing reaches the ERP without my click.

My rule of thumb

  • If the logic can be written down and a wrong answer is expensive, write the rule. It’s cheaper to test, and you can explain it to a manager.
  • If the input is messy language and a person will read the output, use the LLM.
  • If the action is hard to undo, keep a human in the loop — and make approving fast (one keystroke), or people will route around it.
  • Let the model decide only where you have measured it and its mistakes are cheap and reversible.

None of this is anti-AI. It’s the same thinking any process engineer applies to a new machine on the line: put it where it’s strong, guard it where it’s weak, and measure before you trust it.
