AI meets hardware

The AI problems I find most interesting don’t live in a browser. They sit next to a machine: a wire cutting steel, a booth about to be fabricated, a motor pulled out of a discarded appliance.

Software-only AI is having its moment. But most of the economy still runs on physical processes — cutting, painting, curing, moving, assembling — and that’s where I’ve spent my career. Three examples of what I mean:

Research

Quality, predicted while cutting

In our peer-reviewed study, a neural network predicts wire-EDM surface roughness from 12 features of the machine controller’s own voltage, current and feed-rate signals — so a part can be judged during the cut instead of measured afterwards.

Work

Equipment drawings, checked before steel is cut

My drawing checks compare layouts against quotes and vendor drawings — hook loads, ceiling heights, electrical scope — before a booth or an oven is fabricated. A mismatch caught on paper is cheap; caught on site, it isn’t.

Initiative

Recovered motors, new mobility

In the 2026 innovation rally our team pitched EMPUJÓN: a kit that motorises a person’s existing wheelchair with motors recovered from discarded appliances, plus sensors and models to check, map and anticipate failures.

Building

One engineer, real software

With AI coding agents I’ve shipped two production desktop apps used every day. The barrier between “engineer with an idea” and “working tool” has never been lower.

What it takes

  • Knowing the process. You can’t pick the right signal or the right failure mode without understanding the physics and the plant.
  • Designing for asymmetric costs. In hardware, the wrong “accept” can hurt someone or scrap a batch.
  • Keeping people in the loop where safety and expensive mistakes live.
  • Measuring against reality — the part on the profilometer, the decision on record — not just a validation score.

That intersection — process knowledge, software I can ship myself, and AI used where it is strong — is where I want to work.
