From time studies to AI: same method, new tool

I learned to improve processes with a stopwatch: time every step, find the waste, standardise, mistake-proof. I use AI the same way — on the paperwork side of engineering.

In a warehouse in Argentina I timed every process to size the crew, and in a Lean project at Bradley our team found that only 15.5 of 666 lead-time hours of an assembly actually added value. The rest was walking for tools, waiting for parts, picking the wrong fitting and redoing work after a spec change.

Then I became a project engineer and noticed the same wastes on my desk.

The seven wastes, in an engineer’s inbox

Motion

Searching

Digging through threads, folders and ERP screens to find the latest status of a job.

Waiting

Missed signals

An order acknowledgement or a ship-date change buried in a vendor PDF, noticed days later.

Over-processing

Re-typing

Copying the same facts from email into notes, from notes into the ERP.

Defects

Late errors

A drawing that says 350 lb when the quote says 500 lb, caught only after release.

AI as poka-yoke for paperwork

ProjectTracker is essentially a Lean project on my own job: it finds the right project for each email (motion), detects milestones the moment they arrive (waiting), proposes the ERP note so I don’t retype it (over-processing), and checks drawings against quotes before release (defects). The result was ~7.3 hours a week back — and the method was the one I learned with a stopwatch.

The lesson I carry from Lean into AI work: don’t start from the technology. Start from the waste, measure it, and only then pick the tool. Sometimes the tool is a rule. Sometimes it’s a model. Often it’s a better process.
